The mistake people make with a small dining setup is buying a clever folding table and then four bulky chairs you can't do anything with. A drop-leaf table that collapses to six inches against the wall hasn't saved an inch if the chairs still stand around it taking the floor — the chairs are the part that actually crowds a small room, because there are four of them and they're always out. The fix is to treat the seating like the table: chairs that fold flat and hang on a wall or slide behind a door, or that stack into a single column in a corner. Day to day you keep out only the one or two seats you use, and the rest appear when company does. Done right, a dinner-for-four setup lives in the footprint of a dinner-for-one. Here's how folding and stackable chairs compare and the specific sets worth buying.

**For most small spaces, a padded folding dining chair is the best pick — it's comfortable enough to sit through a real meal, yet folds flat to a couple of inches so you can hang it on a wall hook or slide it behind a door and only bring it out when you need the seat.** If you want it to look less like a folding chair, a wood folding pair reads as real furniture; a four-pack covers dinner guests; a modern stackable set stacks into a corner; a café-style metal stacker adds character and works outdoors; and armless stackable chairs slide completely under the table. Below, the best pick for each setup and the one comfort feature worth insisting on.

## How to choose folding & stackable chairs {#choose}

Two choices decide whether fold-away chairs actually get used or end up as uncomfortable backups nobody wants to sit in, and the first is about comfort.

**Insist on a padded seat and a real backrest — or you won't sit in them.** The reason most folding chairs fail is that they're miserable to sit in, so they become the chairs you apologize to a guest for. The ones that work in a small home as everyday dining chairs have a padded seat and a contoured or slatted back that actually supports you, so you can sit through a whole meal without squirming. That comfort is what lets a folding chair replace a standard dining chair rather than just supplement it — which is the entire point in a small space, where you don't have room for both. Wood folding chairs tend to look and feel the most like real furniture; padded metal or fabric ones are lighter and cheaper. Whichever you choose, sit comfort is non-negotiable, because an uncomfortable fold-away chair is just clutter you also can't enjoy.

**Decide how they store — flat on a wall, or stacked in a corner.** Folding and stacking solve the same problem two different ways, and the right one depends on your walls and floor. *Folding* chairs collapse to two or three inches and store vertically: hang them on a wall-mounted hook or rack, slide them behind a door, or tuck them beside the fridge — they take almost no floor and turn unused wall into storage, ideal if your floor is spoken for. *Stacking* chairs don't fold but pile into a single column of four or five in a corner or a closet, which is faster to set up and take down for frequent guests and keeps the chairs looking like normal dining chairs when they're in use. Think about where the chairs will live when they're *not* at the table: a bare wall points to folding, a spare corner points to stacking. Either way you keep out only the seats you use daily and store the rest — the same discipline that makes a [space-saving dining table](/guides/space-saving-dining-tables-small-apartments/) work. For a counter or island instead of a table, the equivalent is a backless stool that tucks under, covered in our [bar stools for small spaces](/guides/best-bar-stools-small-spaces/) guide.

## Common questions {#faq}

### Are folding dining chairs comfortable enough for everyday use?

The good ones are, if you buy for comfort. A folding chair with a genuinely padded seat and a contoured or slatted back supports you through a full meal, and those are the ones that can serve as everyday dining chairs rather than backups. The cheap, flat, all-metal folding chairs are the ones that feel like an event rental and end up unused. Insist on padding and a supportive back, and a folding chair is comfortable enough to live with daily while still collapsing away when you need the floor.

### Folding or stacking chairs — which is better for a small space?

It depends on where the chairs live when they're off the table. Folding chairs collapse to a couple of inches and store vertically — hang them on a wall hook, slide them behind a door — so they're best when your floor is full but you have bare wall. Stacking chairs don't fold but pile into one column in a corner or closet, which is faster for frequent guests and keeps them looking like normal dining chairs in use. Choose folding for wall storage and the smallest footprint, stacking for speed and a more furniture-like look.

### How do I store folding chairs in a small apartment?

Use vertical space and dead gaps. A wall-mounted rack or a few sturdy hooks turn a bare wall into chair storage and keep the floor clear; the narrow gap beside the fridge, a bookshelf, or a wardrobe swallows a folded chair or two; and the back of a closet or pantry door holds several on an over-door rack. Keep out only the one or two chairs you use daily and store the rest, so a table that seats four occupies the everyday footprint of a table for one.

### Can folding or stackable chairs work as the only dining chairs I own?

Yes, and in a small home that's often the smartest setup. If you buy comfortable padded folding or stackable chairs, you can keep one or two out as your daily seats and store the rest, owning a full set of four without ever having four chairs crowding the room. It only works if the chairs are comfortable enough to use every day — a flimsy, hard chair you resent sitting in defeats the purpose — so spend a little more on seat comfort and let the fold-away ability do the space-saving.
