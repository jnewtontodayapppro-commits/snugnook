Open most small-kitchen cabinets and you'll find the same thing. A stack of plates with a foot of empty air above it. Cans buried three-deep where you can't see the back row. An under-sink cabinet that's become a graveyard of half-used sprays around the pipes. The problem usually isn't too few cabinets. It's that each cabinet wastes most of its own volume. Cabinet organizers fix that by carving the dead vertical air into usable tiers and pulling the back of the shelf to the front. Here's how to get a lot more out of a small kitchen, one cabinet at a time, with no renovation and nothing permanent.

**To get more from small-kitchen cabinets, go after the wasted vertical space and the deep back shelves.** Add shelf risers and stacking shelves to use the air above your plates and cans, pull-out drawers and turntables to reach the back of deep cabinets, and an under-sink organizer that works around the plumbing. Racks on the inside of cabinet doors turn dead surfaces into storage. Start with the cabinet that frustrates you most, usually under the sink or the one with the tall empty space above the dishes.

## Use the air above your shelves {#vertical}

The biggest waste in any cabinet is vertical. A short stack of plates or a single row of cans leaves inches of unused air above it, and the fixed shelf can't be moved to reclaim it. Shelf risers and stackable shelf inserts solve this instantly. A riser adds a second tier over your plates or mugs, and a stacking shelf lets you put a row of cans up top and another below in the same slot. It's the same principle as everywhere else in a small home: [go vertical](/guides/make-small-room-look-bigger/), even inside a cabinet. A couple of risers typically turn one crowded shelf into two usable ones, which is about the cheapest way to get more room without a saw.

## Reach the back of deep cabinets {#deep}

Deep cabinets hide their own capacity. Whatever lands at the back becomes invisible and forgotten, so you really only use the front third. Two fixes bring the back to you. A **pull-out cabinet drawer** (a sliding tray that sits on the shelf) lets you glide the whole contents forward, so the back row is one tug away instead of a full unpacking. A **turntable** (lazy Susan) does the same for jars, oils, and condiments: spin it and the back comes around. Corner cabinets, the worst offenders, are exactly where a turntable earns its keep. Both are drop-in and no-install, and they convert dead back-of-cabinet volume into space you'll actually use.

## Tame the under-sink cabinet {#sink}

The under-sink cabinet is small-kitchen storage at its worst, an awkward box with pipes running through it and a damp reputation, but it's also prime real estate going to waste. An under-sink organizer built for the job works *around* the plumbing: two-tier expandable racks with legs that straddle the pipes, or pull-out bins that slide out past them. Add a tension rod across the front to hang spray bottles by their triggers (freeing the floor of the cabinet), and hooks inside the door for brushes and gloves. Suddenly the cleaning-supply chaos has a system, and you can see and reach everything instead of excavating for the dish soap.

## Put the cabinet doors to work {#doors}

The inside of every cabinet door is a flat, vertical, completely unused surface, and it's perfect for the small, flat things that always sprawl. An over-the-door or adhesive-mounted rack turns it into storage for pot lids (which otherwise never stack), cutting boards, foil and wrap boxes, or a row of spices. Under the sink, the door holds sponges and gloves. By the trash, it holds the bag roll. Because a door rack uses space that was doing literally nothing, it's about the highest-return few dollars you can spend in a cramped kitchen. Just check the door still shuts once it's loaded.

## Corral cans, spices &amp; the pantry cabinet {#pantry}

If you don't have a pantry, and most small kitchens don't, one cabinet has to be one, and organizers make it work. A tiered can rack or a can dispenser stops the buried-back-row problem and shows you what you have at a glance. A stepped spice shelf or an in-drawer spice insert ends the rummaging through a jumble of jars. Clear stackable bins group snacks, baking supplies, or grains so a whole category pulls out together. The goal is that the pantry cabinet reads like a small store shelf, everything visible and everything grouped, instead of a pile you dig through. That's also how you stop buying a third jar of cumin you didn't know you had.

## Common cabinet-organizing mistakes {#mistakes}

Three to avoid. **Ignoring the vertical air.** The single biggest waste in a cabinet is the empty space above a short stack, so risers and stacking shelves are the first buy, not an afterthought. **Buying before measuring.** Cabinet interiors, and the gaps around under-sink pipes, vary a lot, so measure width, depth, and height (and pipe positions) before ordering, or you'll be returning organizers that don't fit. And **organizing without curating.** No rack fixes a cabinet crammed with duplicate gadgets and expired cans, so thin the contents first, then size the organizers to what's left.

## Common questions {#faq}

Together these can get close to doubling a cabinet's usable capacity, depending on how much dead air you're starting with.

A stepped spice shelf or in-drawer insert ends the jar rummage. Clear stackable bins group snacks, grains, or baking supplies so each category pulls out as a unit.
