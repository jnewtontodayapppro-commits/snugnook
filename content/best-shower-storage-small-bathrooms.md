The tub edge lined with bottles is the small-bathroom mess everyone knows. There's nowhere else to put the shampoo, so it collects on the ledge, on the floor, on the soap dish, until the shower feels half its size. The fix is cheap and takes ten minutes: get everything up onto something that drains, holds its weight, and won't rust a streak down your tile. The trick is matching the caddy to your shower — a showerhead to hang from, a corner to brace into, or a wall to stick to — and buying rustproof so it lasts more than a season. Here are the ones worth owning.

**For most small showers, a rustproof caddy that hangs over the showerhead is the best pick — it needs no drilling, no suction cups, and holds the everyday bottles up and out of the way.** If you have a lot to store, a tension pole braces into the corner from floor to ceiling; if you want shelves exactly where you want them, adhesive corner shelves stick to tile with no tools. Below, the best of each and what to avoid.

## How to choose a shower caddy {#choose}

Two decisions do most of the work: how it mounts, and what it's made of.

**Pick a mount that fits your shower.** A hanging caddy loops over the showerhead and is the fastest, most renter-safe option, but it puts the weight on the pipe, so it suits a moderate load, not your whole collection. A tension pole wedges between the floor of the tub and the ceiling and holds the most, which makes it the answer for a shared bathroom with a lot of bottles. Adhesive shelves stick to the tile wherever you want them and stay out of the way, as long as you respect their weight limit. Match the mount to the surface you actually have.

**Buy rustproof, or buy twice.** A steamy shower destroys cheap metal. A wire caddy that isn't truly rustproof will streak orange down your tub or grout within a season, and in a rental that stain is exactly the kind that costs a deposit. Look for aluminum, 304 stainless, or a quality rustproof coating, and treat anything vague about its material as a no. This is the one spec worth paying a few dollars more for.

**Skip suction cups for anything with weight.** Suction lets go — usually at 6 a.m. with a full bottle of shampoo, and it clatters into the tub and wakes the house. Humidity and temperature swings beat the seal over time. Lean on hanging caddies, tension poles, and strong adhesive instead; if a product's main selling point is its suction cups, keep scrolling. ## Common questions {#faq}

### What's the best shower caddy that won't rust?

Look for aluminum or 304 stainless steel, or a caddy explicitly sold as rustproof — that's the spec that decides whether it lasts. A hanging aluminum caddy over the showerhead is the easiest rustproof option and needs no tools. Avoid cheap uncoated wire, which streaks orange down the tub within a season and can cost a renter their deposit.

### Do adhesive shower shelves actually hold?

Yes, within their limits. A quality adhesive corner shelf on clean, dry tile holds a couple of bottles just fine — the failures come from overloading it or loading it before the glue cures. Wipe the tile with rubbing alcohol, press the shelf on, and give it a full 24 hours before you put anything on it. Send the heavy stuff to a hanging caddy or tension pole instead.

### Why shouldn't I use a suction-cup caddy?

Because it lets go. Suction seals weaken with heat, humidity, and time, and a loaded suction caddy tends to fall at the worst moment and clatter into the tub. For anything holding weight, a caddy that hangs from the showerhead, braces as a tension pole, or sticks with strong adhesive is far more reliable. Save suction for a lightweight razor holder at most.

### How do I store shower stuff in a rental without drilling?

Everything in this guide is no-drill. Hang a caddy over the showerhead, brace a tension pole into the corner, or stick adhesive shelves to the tile — all of it comes down clean on move-out day. Pair it with a freestanding rack over the toilet and you've got a fully stored bathroom without a single hole in the wall.
