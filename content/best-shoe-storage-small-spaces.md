Shoes are uniquely good at wrecking a small space. They pile up right where you walk in, they sprawl across the closet floor, and a family's worth of them can bury an entryway in a week. The problem isn't usually *how many* shoes you own — it's that they have no designated home, so they colonize the floor. The fix is to give them a defined, vertical, out-of-the-way spot sized to what you actually wear. Here's how to store shoes in a small apartment, whether your problem is the entryway, the closet, or both.

**The best shoe storage for a small space goes vertical or hidden:** an over-the-door organizer or a hanging rack to use the back of a door, a slim vertical shoe cabinet or bench to contain them behind a front, and stackable racks or under-bed boxes for the closet. Keep only the current season by the door and store the rest — no organizer beats simply not keeping every pair within reach.

## The one rule: go vertical or hidden {#principle}

Shoes on the floor read as clutter, full stop — a visible pile is what makes an entryway or closet feel chaotic even when nothing else is out of place. So the governing rule of small-space shoe storage is simple: get them off the floor and out of sight. That means using vertical real estate (the back of a door, the height of a wall) or hiding them behind a front (a cabinet, a bench with a lid). Every solution below is a version of that one idea. Once the shoes have a designated vertical or hidden home, the space around them instantly reads calmer.

## Use the back of the door {#door}

The back of your entryway or closet door is prime storage doing nothing, and it's the fastest shoe fix there is. An over-the-door shoe organizer with pockets hangs in seconds and holds a dozen or more pairs on space you weren't using, with zero floor cost and no tools — the same trick from our [over-the-door organizer guide](/guides/best-over-the-door-organizers-small-apartments/) applied to footwear. A hanging shoe rack on the closet rod does the same for a closet. This is the highest-return, lowest-effort move for a small entryway: it clears the floor pile immediately and comes down clean when you move.

## Slim cabinets &amp; benches {#cabinet}

When you want shoes *hidden* rather than just off the floor, a slim shoe cabinet is the answer. These are shallow units — often just ten to fifteen inches deep — with tilt-out drawers that hold shoes on edge, so a whole collection disappears behind a clean front along an entryway wall. A shoe storage bench does double duty: you sit on it to put shoes on while they're stored inside, retiring both a rack and a hall chair at once. Both keep the shoes contained and the entryway looking tidy, which matters most in the spot that's the first thing you see coming home.

## Closet &amp; under-bed storage {#closet}

For the shoes that aren't in daily rotation, the closet and the space under your bed do the heavy lifting. A stackable shoe rack turns the closet floor's wasted vertical air into two or three tiers, and clear stackable shoe boxes protect and display pairs while stacking up a closet wall. Under the bed, a low shoe organizer or a drawer on wheels swallows off-season and special-occasion pairs entirely — the same under-bed real estate we cover in our [under-bed storage guide](/guides/best-under-bed-storage-containers/), pointed at footwear. Send the shoes you rarely wear to these spots so the entryway only holds what you're actually using this week.

## Curate before you store {#curate}

Before buying a single organizer, do the free step: pull every pair and be honest about what you wear. Most people keep shoes they haven't touched in a year, and no storage system beats simply owning fewer. Keep the current season's rotation accessible by the door, box or store the off-season pairs, and let go of the ones you don't wear at all. Curating first often reveals that the entryway wasn't too small — it was just holding three times the shoes it needed to. Then size your storage to the pairs that are left, not the pile you started with.

## Common shoe-storage mistakes {#mistakes}

Three to avoid. **Leaving shoes on the floor** — a visible pile is the whole problem, so get them vertical or hidden. **Storing every pair by the door** — keep only the current season in the entryway and send the rest to the closet or under the bed, or any rack overflows in days. And **buying storage before curating** — organizers can't fix owning more shoes than you have room for, so thin the collection first, then size the storage to what remains.

## Common questions {#faq}

### How do I store shoes in a small apartment?

Get them off the floor and out of sight: hang an over-the-door organizer to use the back of a door, add a slim shoe cabinet or storage bench to hide them behind a front along the entryway, and send off-season pairs to stackable closet racks or under-bed boxes. Keep only the current season's shoes by the door and store the rest, so the entryway holds what you actually wear.

### What's the best shoe storage for a small entryway?

For most entryways, a slim shoe cabinet (shallow, with tilt-out drawers) hides the collection behind a clean front while taking up little depth, or a storage bench lets you sit to put shoes on with them stored inside. If floor space is truly gone, an over-the-door organizer clears the pile using space you weren't using at all. Match it to whether you want the shoes hidden or just off the floor.

### How can I store shoes without taking up floor space?

Go vertical and hidden. The back of a door holds a dozen pairs in an over-the-door organizer; a hanging rack uses the closet rod; stackable racks climb the closet floor's vertical air; and under-bed boxes swallow off-season pairs entirely. Combining a door organizer for daily shoes with under-bed storage for the rest keeps footwear off the floor completely.
