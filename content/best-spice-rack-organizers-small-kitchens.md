Spices are the worst storage problem in a small kitchen relative to their size. A dozen jars take up a shelf, a drawer, or a stretch of counter, and because they're stored standing in a crowd, you can't read the labels on the back row — so you buy a second jar of cumin you already own and the crowd grows. The fix isn't a bigger kitchen; it's giving the spices a dedicated spot where every label faces you and nothing sits behind anything. That spot can be a wall, the inside of a cabinet door, a drawer, or a corner of the counter — the right one depends on where *your* small kitchen has slack. Here's how the six styles compare and the specific organizer worth buying for each.

**For most small kitchens, a wall-mounted or cabinet-door spice rack is the best pick — it moves the whole spice collection off the counter and out of the cabinet onto vertical space you weren't using, with every label visible.** If you have a spare drawer, an in-drawer insert lays spices flat and labels-up; a countertop lazy Susan spins them into reach; magnetic tins stick to the fridge or a wall plate; and a pull-out rack turns a deep cabinet into a spice drawer. Below, the best pick for each spot and how to figure out which one your kitchen has room for.

## How to choose a spice organizer {#choose}

The whole decision comes down to one question — where does your kitchen have empty space? — plus a rule about how you actually reach for spices.

**Find your slack, then match the rack to it.** A small kitchen almost always has one kind of unused space and not the others. If your walls or the backs of cabinet doors are bare, go vertical: a wall rack or a door-mounted rack adds a full column of spice storage without touching a shelf or the counter, and the inside of one standard cabinet door holds roughly 18 to 30 spice jars on a six-tier rack. If a drawer is the thing you can spare, an in-drawer insert is the tidiest option there is — spices lie at a 30- to 45-degree angle so you read every label looking straight down, and the drawer closes over them so nothing shows. If neither is free but you have a few inches of counter or a deep cabinet shelf, a lazy Susan or a pull-out rack brings the back row forward. Buy for the space you have, not the setup you saw in a photo.

**Count your jars and measure before you buy.** This is the step people skip, and it's why racks arrive too small or don't fit. Count your spice jars — most home cooks have between 12 and 24 — and buy a rack rated for at least that many, because a spice collection only grows. Then measure the actual spot: the free width on a wall, the flat inner panel of the cabinet door (avoid the raised frame), the inside depth of the drawer (many drawer inserts need a drawer at least 3 inches deep), or the shelf clearance for a two-tier turntable. A tape measure spent up front beats a return.

**Decant, or at least standardize, if you want the full space win.** Racks are sized for uniform jars, and a shelf of mismatched grocery-store bottles — tall, short, round, square — wastes the space between them and topples on a turntable. You don't have to decant everything into matching jars, but the organizers that hold the most do it because every jar is the same footprint. If you're already reorganizing, uniform jars plus a label system is what turns a rack from "better" into "you can find any spice in two seconds." This is the same everything-visible principle that runs through our [small kitchen storage guide](/guides/small-kitchen-storage-ideas/) — spices are just its hardest case. And once the spices are handled, the [dish drying rack](/guides/best-dish-drying-racks-small-kitchens/) is usually the next counter hog worth solving.

## Common questions {#faq}

### What is the best way to store spices in a small kitchen?

Get them off the flat surfaces and onto vertical or hidden space. A wall rack or a cabinet-door rack moves the whole collection off the counter and out of the cabinet interior, and an in-drawer insert lays them flat and labels-up in a spare drawer. The goal in a small kitchen is that every spice is visible without moving another one — that's what stops you buying duplicates and losing jars at the back. Pick the style that matches where your kitchen happens to have empty space.

### How do I organize spices without any counter or cabinet space?

Use the surfaces everyone forgets: the inside of a cabinet door and the side of the fridge. A six-tier over-the-door or door-mounted rack turns a bare door panel into a full column of spice storage, and magnetic tins stick to the fridge's side or a steel wall plate for a truly zero-footprint setup. Both add real spice capacity without borrowing a single inch of shelf or counter — the exact move for a kitchen where everything else is already full.

### Are in-drawer spice organizers worth it?

If you can spare a shallow drawer, they're the tidiest option there is. The insert tilts each jar so you read every label looking straight down, you reach any spice in one motion, and the drawer closes over the whole thing so nothing shows on the counter. The catch is you need a free drawer that's deep enough — many inserts want at least three inches — and jars of a fairly uniform size to sit neatly. Where those line up, an in-drawer system is hard to beat.

### How many spice jars should a rack hold?

Buy for more than you own now. Most home cooks keep somewhere between 12 and 24 spices, and a collection only grows, so a rack rated for around 20 to 30 jars leaves room without being oversized. Count what you have, add some margin, and check the listing's stated capacity against that number — a rack that's already full the day it arrives is one you'll outgrow in a month.

### Should I decant spices into matching jars?

You don't have to, but it's what unlocks the most space. Racks and drawer inserts are sized for uniform jars, so a shelf of mismatched grocery bottles wastes the gaps between them and topples on a turntable. Decanting into matching jars with labels makes everything the same footprint, packs more into the same rack, and lets you find any spice instantly. If you're reorganizing anyway, it's the step that turns a rack from a little better into genuinely effortless.
