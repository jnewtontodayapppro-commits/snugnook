Rental windows are a quiet misery: the landlord's cheap plastic mini-blinds are bent and yellowing, or there's nothing on the glass at all and you're dressing with the lights off. And the lease says no holes, so the obvious fix — screwing in a proper bracket — is off the table. The good news is that window coverings have quietly gone no-drill. Cellular shades now come with tension or adhesive brackets that need no tools, whole shades stick straight to the window frame, and you can even get motorized blackout blinds that mount without a single screw. Done right, a rental window goes from eyesore to finished in twenty minutes, and the whole thing lifts off clean on move-out day. Here's how the styles compare and the specific shades worth buying.

**For most rental windows, a no-drill cordless cellular (honeycomb) shade is the best pick — it mounts with tension or adhesive brackets in minutes with no tools, insulates the drafty window a rental always has, and comes in blackout or light-filtering to suit any room.** If you want style, a zebra shade gives adjustable light and a roman shade adds fabric softness; for the fastest fix, a stick-on shade needs no bracket at all; for a bedroom, a motorized blackout runs on a remote; and a light-filtering cellular keeps privacy without the dark. Below, the best pick for each window and the one measurement that makes or breaks the fit.

## How to choose no-drill window coverings {#choose}

Three things decide whether a no-drill shade looks custom or falls in your lap at 2 a.m., and the first is a tape measure.

**Measure for an inside mount — width at three points, and take the smallest.** Almost every no-drill shade mounts *inside* the window frame, held by tension or small adhesive brackets, so it has to fit the opening precisely. Measure the width at the top, middle, and bottom of the frame — rental windows are rarely square — and order to the *narrowest* of the three, because a shade cut to the widest point won't fit the narrow one. Measure the height on both sides and take the longer. Check the inside depth too: tension and adhesive brackets need an inch or two of flat frame to grip, and a shallow frame may force an outside (adhesive-to-the-wall) mount instead. Five minutes with a tape measure is the difference between a shade that looks built-in and an expensive return.

**Pick your mount for the surface — and the deposit.** No-drill shades hold three ways, and the right one depends on your frame. *Tension* brackets wedge inside the frame with spring pressure and leave nothing behind at all — the most deposit-safe option, best for a snug, square opening. *Adhesive* brackets stick to the frame or wall with 3M-style tape and hold more weight and wider shades, but test them on a hidden spot first, because on cheap or freshly painted frames the tape can lift paint. *Stick-on* shades skip brackets entirely and adhere the whole headrail to the glass or frame — the fastest install and fine for light shades. Whatever the mount, warm the adhesive with a hair dryer at move-out and peel slowly, and it comes away clean.

**Match the opacity to the room — blackout, light-filtering, or both.** This is the choice people regret getting wrong. *Blackout* (or room-darkening) shades block light for a bedroom or a studio where the bed is three feet from the window — essential for shift workers and anyone facing a streetlight. *Light-filtering* shades give daytime privacy while letting soft light through, right for a living room or kitchen where you want brightness, not a cave. A zebra or day-night shade splits the difference, rolling between sheer and opaque on demand. Cellular (honeycomb) shades also trap air in their pockets, which genuinely cuts drafts and energy bills at the thin, leaky window a rental always seems to have — a side benefit worth having. ## Common questions {#faq}

### How do I cover a rental window without drilling?

Use a no-drill shade that mounts by tension or adhesive. Cordless cellular shades wedge inside the frame with spring-tension brackets or stick on with 3M-style adhesive brackets, both tool-free and removable; stick-on pleated shades adhere the whole shade straight to the frame with no bracket at all. For curtains, pair a tension or magnetic rod with your shade. All of it goes up without a drill and comes off clean, so your deposit stays safe while the window finally looks finished.

### Do no-drill blinds actually stay up?

Yes, when they're matched to the window. Tension-mounted shades hold by spring pressure inside a snug, square frame and stay put for light to medium shades; adhesive brackets hold more weight and wider shades as long as the surface is clean and you let the tape cure before loading them. The failures come from a frame that's too shallow for the brackets to grip, a shade ordered too wide for the opening, or hanging a heavy shade on light adhesive. Measure carefully and match the mount to the weight and they stay up for years.

### What's the difference between blackout and light-filtering shades?

Blackout (room-darkening) shades block essentially all outside light — the right choice for a bedroom, a studio where you sleep near the window, or anyone bothered by a streetlight. Light-filtering shades give you daytime privacy while letting soft, diffused light through, so a living room or kitchen stays bright but screened from view. A zebra or day-night shade adjusts between the two on demand. Decide by the room: darkness where you sleep, filtered light where you live.

### Will adhesive window shades damage the paint or frame?

They can on cheap or freshly painted frames, so test first. Most no-drill adhesive brackets use a 3M-style tape that releases cleanly if you remove it slowly and warm it with a hair dryer, but paint that's new (give it two to three weeks to cure) or low-quality can lift with the tape. Stick one bracket in a hidden corner, wait a day, and peel it to check before mounting the whole shade. If the frame looks risky, use tension-mounted shades instead, which grip by pressure and leave nothing behind at all.
